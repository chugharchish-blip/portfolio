Executive Summary

Situation

A bank-led group of lenders had taken a borrower to the insolvency tribunal, with ₹347 crore of exposure.

Challenge

Ten years of records (2008–2018) across 13 entities, with many flows set up to look like normal trade.

Approach

Checked how loans drawn by the CEO, CTO and directors were used against the sanction letters, traced fund flows, and tested related-party disclosures.

Result

Over ₹200 crore found diverted. Fake purchase and billing entries mapped as evidence. Evidence pack and control recommendations given to the lenders.

01

Background

The lenders had classified the account as non-performing and started insolvency proceedings. They suspected that loan money had been moved into companies connected to the promoters rather than used for the purposes it was sanctioned for. Every finding had to be backed by a document, as it would be used before the tribunal.

The records were spread across a decade of audited financial statements, annual reports, bank statements, sanction letters and corporate registry filings for 13 entities. Many of the flows had been set up to look like ordinary trade.

02

Approach

  1. Phase 1PlanHypotheses, sanction terms and the entity universe
  2. Phase 2FieldworkTransaction dataset, end-use and trade-substance testing
  3. Phase 3ReportFindings, fund-flow charts and a referenced evidence pack
  4. Phase 4RemediateControl recommendations for the lenders
  1. Scope and hypothesesSet out the lenders' concerns as testable hypotheses: diversion, siphoning, round-tripping and undisclosed related parties. Collected sanction letters and loan agreements to fix the permitted end use.
  2. Entity universeUsed corporate registry research to link directors, shareholders and addresses, and grew the network from the borrower to 13 connected entities.
  3. Build the dataConverted ten years of bank statements and financials into one transaction dataset, tagged by entity, counterparty, amount and date.
  4. End-use testingMatched each large drawdown, including loans drawn by the CEO, CTO and directors, to its sanctioned purpose, and flagged outflows that went to group companies soon after disbursement.
  5. Trade substanceTested purchase and sales entries for real goods movement: invoices with no delivery trail, circular billing, and counterparties with no operations.
  6. Disclosure checkCompared the related-party transactions found with what the annual reports disclosed under SEBI rules and IAS 24 principles.
  7. Evidence packPut together fund-flow charts, a transaction schedule and a document index, each linked to a source document, for the lenders and their counsel.

05

Control Gaps and Recommendations

  • End-use monitoring: lenders relied on the borrower's own certificates rather than checking where the money actually went after disbursement.
  • Related-party identification: no independent check of shared directors, shareholders or addresses across counterparties.
  • Trade substance: invoices were accepted without checking delivery documents, GST filings or whether the counterparty actually operated.
  • Early-warning signals: circular flows and sudden growth in inter-company balances did not trigger a review.
  • Board oversight: related-party transactions went through without proper audit committee approval or disclosure.

06

Outcome

The lenders received an evidence pack, with every entry linked to a source document, to support their case in the insolvency proceedings. They also received recommendations to tighten end-use monitoring and related-party checks on other accounts.

Key Takeaways

  1. Borrower certificates do not prove end use. The money has to be traced to where it went.
  2. Registry data (shared directors, shareholders and addresses) shows related parties that disclosures leave out.
  3. Fake billing shows up as invoices with no delivery trail behind them.
UAE

Relevance to UAE Roles

CBUAE-regulated banks run the same end-use and early-warning controls tested here, and UAE bankruptcy law allows creditors to challenge similar transactions. This work is relevant to fraud investigation, credit review and AML roles in UAE banks.
