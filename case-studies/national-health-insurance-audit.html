01 · Context

A large public scheme, with public money at risk

National health insurance schemes pay empanelled hospitals for treating registered beneficiaries. Each step, from enrolment to admission, pre-authorisation, claim, payout and the hospital's own purchasing, is a point where money can be taken. The authority wanted an independent view of where its controls were failing and who was benefiting.

02 · Risk-based approach

Planning, fieldwork and reporting

  1. PlanningMapped the claims cycle and the providers' procurement cycle. Ranked the risks by likelihood and impact, and put fieldwork time into the highest-risk providers and processes rather than spreading it evenly.
  2. Data analyticsTested the full claims population for red flags: identity mismatches, impossible treatment patterns, clusters of claims from one referral source, and directors who also appeared on vendor records.
  3. FieldworkChecked patient records against registrations, followed payments through intercompany deposits and counterparty records, and verified whether vendors really existed and operated.
  4. Root causeFor each finding, identified the control that failed (verification, segregation, disclosure or vendor due diligence) and explained how it was bypassed.
  5. ReportingRated the findings, supported each with evidence, and gave practical recommendations. Worked with the authority on what to do first.

06 · Impact

Safeguarding public funds

The fake vendor network found in the review was later shut down. The findings gave the authority evidence to act against the providers involved, and a list of control changes to stop the same schemes happening again across its network.

  • Stronger identity checks at enrolment and admission
  • Required disclosure of referral arrangements and director conflicts
  • Vendor due diligence and procure-to-pay controls for empanelled providers
  • Analytics-based red flags for ongoing claims monitoring
Relevance to UAE roles

How this carries over to the UAE

Mandatory health insurance in Dubai and Abu Dhabi has the same exposure points: provider fraud, referral incentives and upcoding. DHA and DoH Abu Dhabi expect insurers, TPAs and providers to run fraud controls. This review is directly relevant to payer fraud units, insurer internal audit and healthcare compliance roles.
